\section{Sufficiency-Aware Evaluation of HPC Systems}
\label{sec:plan-evaluation}

The mapping study in \Cref{ch:mapping} showed that evaluation in the field is focused on operational efficiency. The Green500 ranks supercomputers by performance per watt, measured on a single benchmark \parencite{feng2007Green500}. That metric gave the field a shared target beyond raw speed, and the list has run for fifteen years on it \parencite{adhinarayanan2025Looking}. A ratio of one impact to one measure of useful work still leaves most of a system's footprint outside the frame. Karanikolaou and Bekakos \parencite{karanikolaou2022Action} find systems of entirely different size and capability sitting on consecutive rows of the Green500. Rao and Chien \parencite{rao2025Modeling} put numbers on the same effect in the Top500. Leonardo emits 4.3 times the operational carbon of LUMI, the system ranked directly above it, and Frontier carries 2.6 times the embodied carbon of El Capitan. Absolute power draw, hardware composition, the carbon intensity of the local grid, and the water spent generating that electricity all separate those machines, and a performance-per-watt ratio reports none of it.

The aim is to use the ranking as a reference point and show what a sufficiency-aware lens reveals once that ranking is held against the rest of a system's footprint. Efficiency measures how much useful work a system returns per unit of impact, a ratio that improves as the numerator grows. Sufficiency concerns something else: whether the total burden a system imposes, and the growth in demand behind it, can be brought down at all \parencite{santarius2022Digital}. A relative metric can improve while the absolute quantity it divides keeps climbing, which is how a fleet grows greener per operation and heavier in total at the same time \parencite{york2022Rebound}. The Top500 already sits in that position. Performance per unit of carbon keeps improving while the fleet's operational carbon grows by 10.3 percent a year, on track to nearly double between 2024 and 2030 \parencite{rao2025Modeling}. The same arithmetic holds outside the machine room. Yang and Wang \parencite{yang2023Impact} compare a Chinese national supercomputing center against a synthetic control city and find that it worsened regional carbon emissions, because growth in energy demand outweighed what the center returned through a cleaner energy structure or greener technology. We propose a diagnostic lens that surfaces what efficiency-only evaluation hides, a step towards deciding how much computation is justified.

We plan to approach this gradually. Each step moves further from efficiency toward sufficiency, evaluating what it reveals, what data it demands, and what it trades away. The first step keeps the shape of a ranking but widens the impacts it accounts for. Beyond performance per watt alone, systems will be ranked according to the impact dimensions presented by the mapping. Carbon modelling already reaches both the operational and the manufacturing stage \parencite{li2023Sustainable}. Water can be estimated from the electricity a system draws and from the scarcity of the region supplying it \parencite{jiang2025ThirstyFLOPS}. The exercise is modest and near-term, since it needs only public Top500 entries and published impact factors. We expect that the rankings disagree once the impacts are introduced. A ranking on more dimensions is still a relative comparison, still efficiency thinking with a wider lens.

The second step will introduce sufficiency-oriented indicators, including, but not limited to, its total lifecycle burden, how heavily it is actually used, and how often it is replaced. They are also where the efficiency account and the sufficiency account diverge. The challenge of this step is data. Rao and Chien \parencite{rao2025Modeling} found a utilization figure for three of the 500 ranked systems, and essentially none of them report actual power draw, so we expect resorting to estimations and modelling. More than providing numbers, we intend to provide guidelines on how to make this information more accessible.

The third step turns the missing data into the object of study. Rather than rank systems only as they stand, we will test what the ranking becomes under a change: the same machine on a cleaner grid, with a newer generation of hardware, under a less water-intensive cooling design, or against a rising demand trajectory. The levers come from work already done, such as the balance between embodied and operational carbon that governs when hardware should be replaced \parencite{li2023Sustainable, uwizeyimana2025Carbonaware}. Each scenario supplies an assumed figure where the real one is unavailable, then shows how the re-ranking responds. We expect some levers to move a system's standing and others to barely register. The scenarios also let us argue for data the field does not yet publish, since they show what conclusions that data would support.

A boundary runs through all three steps. Public data supports part of this evaluation and not the rest. Peak performance and rated power are reported for most ranked systems, while embodied carbon per machine, the water intensity of a specific site, and true utilization are not. Rao and Chien \parencite{rao2025Modeling} mark roughly where the line falls, covering 98 percent of the Top500 for operational carbon once public sources beyond the list are added, and 80.8 percent for embodied carbon. We treat drawing that line as a result of its own. It marks how far a sufficiency-aware evaluation reaches on today's public record, and it turns the gap into a concrete request to the operators and vendors who could close it. The what-if step keeps the boundary from being a dead end, since it shows the payoff of each missing figure before anyone has measured it.

Taken together, the three steps move from a wider efficiency ranking toward an account of absolute burden. They rest on the same foundation as the rest of this thesis, the map of \Cref{ch:mapping} and the premise that HPC's impacts run past energy efficiency. The Green500 gave HPC a way to reward efficient machines. We aim to give the field a way to see what efficiency alone leaves out, and to make how much computing is enough part of how systems are judged.
