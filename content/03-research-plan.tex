\chapter{Research Plan}
\label{ch:plan}

This chapter presents the research plan through the thesis defense. \Cref{sec:plan-operations} presents what to expect from the job shifting experiments and how we plan to develop the mechanisms involving the scheduler, users, platform controls, and hardware replacement. \Cref{sec:plan-evaluation} presents the sufficiency-aware evaluation of public machine data.

\section{Accounting for Environmental Cost in HPC Operations}
\label{sec:plan-operations}

An HPC center's cost is decided in four places. The scheduler chooses when and where each job runs from queue state and node availability. Users submit work and are charged in core-hours. The platform runs its nodes at prespecified power and frequency settings. Hardware is replaced on a cycle set by performance and procurement. Environmental cost rarely enters any of these decisions.

Greenfilling showed what is not enough. Refusing a start when the current intensity sits above its moving average buys a certain delay for a return that depends on intensities the deferred job may never meet. \Cref{ch:preliminary} reports the outcome: no scenario median moved the targeted footprint by 0.1\%, and mean bounded slowdown rose in all 288 pairs. A mechanism therefore has to price both sides of the decision it makes, the impact of acting now against the impact of waiting.

The rest of this section adds one actor at a time. The first step leaves the scheduler alone with an exogenous grid signal, as Greenfilling did. The second brings in users, since a center's footprint depends on what is submitted and not only on how it is placed. The third brings in the platform, by turning its power state into something a scheduler can choose, and the fourth extends the platform through time, to aging and replacement.

The first step closes the job-shifting arc. \Cref{eq:shifting-cost} prices a single job in isolation, and the offline analysis of \Cref{ch:preliminary} used it to bound what shifting can reach, 6.76\% of operational carbon and 4.89\% of water at a maximum displacement of 24 hours. That bound is unrestricted: it lets every one of the 22.2\% of displaced jobs claim the same low-intensity valleys at once, and it assumes the whole intensity trace is known at the moment of the decision. Implementing the cost function as a Batsched scheduler removes the first assumption, since node capacity then decides which jobs actually reach the valleys they price. We will evaluate it against EASY and both Greenfilling variants on the campaign design of \Cref{tab:greenfilling-campaign}, sweeping $d_m$, and report what fraction of the offline bound survives contention and what that fraction costs in bounded slowdown. Wiesner et al. \parencite{wiesner2021Lets} report savings from temporal shifting in the cloud, and Sukprasert et al. \parencite{sukprasert2024Limitations} report how quickly those savings shrink once deadlines and workload structure are respected. HPC batch queues sit closer to the second setting than to the first, so we expect the realized saving to fall short of the bound. Measuring how far short and why is the point of the step.

The second assumption is harder to remove, since a scheduler deciding now cannot integrate over a trace it has not seen. The ENTSO-E platform the campaign draws its signals from also publishes day-ahead generation forecasts \parencite{hirth2018ENTSOE}, so the question becomes how much of the realized saving survives when the cost function integrates a forecast instead of the recorded signal. If the saving holds under forecast error the mechanism is deployable, and if it disappears then the ceiling was an artifact of hindsight. This step completes the mechanism left half-built in \Cref{ch:preliminary}.

The second step makes the platform an actor. The platform model so far holds active power constant, so reordering the same jobs cannot change active energy. The only levers left are idle periods and the intensities a job meets while it runs. Lifting that assumption changes the decision itself. \Cref{eq:shifting-cost} currently prices only a job's start time. Adding power state as a second decision variable lets the scheduler price both together and trade execution time against power as well as delay.

That poses a question the earlier steps cannot ask: does slowing a job down beat making it wait? Running at a lower frequency stretches a job across more of the trace and lowers its draw throughout, while waiting preserves the draw and moves it. Neither is obviously better, and which one wins should depend on the shape of the signal and on how a job's runtime responds to frequency. The available knobs are node power states, as well as per-node DVFS and RAPL power caps \parencite{david2010RAPL}. EcoFreq \parencite{kozlov2024EcoFreq} scales power against carbon and price signals, and it gives the mechanism a reference point.

Cooling provides a second control surface beyond the compute nodes. The footprint model already supports time-varying PUE and WUE, but the Greenfilling campaign fixed these values at 1 and 0, leaving this capability unused. Making cooling a decision variable would require a thermal model that the testbed currently lacks, so we will prioritize compute controls first. We will investigate cooling controls if the results show that the trade-off between execution time and power consumption justifies the additional model complexity.

The third step leaves the operational stage. Replacement timing decides how much manufacturing impact a center commits to and how long it has to amortize that impact. Li et al. \parencite{li2023Sustainable} and Uwizeyimana and Enright Jerger \parencite{uwizeyimana2025Carbonaware} frame the decision as a crossover: a newer machine lowers operational impact per unit of work and adds embodied impact at once, so replacing early spends carbon to save carbon, and the balance depends on how the machine is used.

Replacement decision plays out over years. We will build a long-horizon simulation regime for it, running a machine's service life at coarser resolution, with aging modeled directly. The simulator already carries embodied carbon and water per host, so those values become the quantity a replacement policy reasons about, amortized across the work the machine actually delivers. We will compare three policies over a fixed horizon. A fixed cycle replaces the machine every N years, which is what centers usually do. A utilization trigger replaces it once a usage metric crosses a threshold. A crossover policy replaces it when the operational impact a newer machine would save exceeds the embodied impact of manufacturing it.

The crossover weighs a new machine's embodied impact against the operational impact the current one keeps accruing. Prior work computes that second quantity from a fixed workload. A footprint-aware scheduler changes it. If the first three steps lower operational impact, they push the crossover later and extend the service life the balance justifies, which ties the operational mechanisms to the lifecycle decision instead of leaving the two independent. The same regime reaches past replacement. Repurposing decommissioned hardware \parencite{lynar2010Clustering} and the disposal costs the field is beginning to quantify \parencite{zhao2025HighPowered} are the questions after it.

\section{Sufficiency-Aware Evaluation of HPC Systems}
\label{sec:plan-evaluation}

The mapping study in \Cref{ch:mapping} showed that evaluation in the field is focused on operational efficiency. The Green500 ranks supercomputers by performance per watt, measured on a single benchmark \parencite{feng2007Green500}. That metric gave the field a shared target beyond raw speed, and the list has run for fifteen years on it \parencite{adhinarayanan2025Looking}. A ratio of one impact to one measure of useful work still leaves most of a system's footprint outside the frame. Karanikolaou and Bekakos \parencite{karanikolaou2022Action} find systems of entirely different size and capability sitting on consecutive rows of the Green500. Rao and Chien \parencite{rao2025Modeling} put numbers on the same effect in the Top500. Leonardo emits 4.3 times the operational carbon of LUMI, the system ranked directly above it, and Frontier carries 2.6 times the embodied carbon of El Capitan. Absolute power draw, hardware composition, the carbon intensity of the local grid, and the water spent generating that electricity all separate those machines, and a performance-per-watt ratio reports none of it.

The aim is to use the ranking as a reference point and show what a sufficiency-aware lens reveals once that ranking is held against the rest of a system's footprint. Efficiency measures how much useful work a system returns per unit of impact, a ratio that improves as the numerator grows. Sufficiency concerns something else: whether the total burden a system imposes, and the growth in demand behind it, can be brought down at all \parencite{santarius2022Digital}. A relative metric can improve while the absolute quantity it divides keeps climbing, which is how a fleet grows greener per operation and heavier in total at the same time \parencite{york2022Rebound}. The Top500 already sits in that position. Performance per unit of carbon keeps improving while the fleet's operational carbon grows by 10.3 percent a year, on track to nearly double between 2024 and 2030 \parencite{rao2025Modeling}. The same arithmetic holds outside the machine room. Yang and Wang \parencite{yang2023Impact} compare a Chinese national supercomputing center against a synthetic control city and find that it worsened regional carbon emissions, because growth in energy demand outweighed what the center returned through a cleaner energy structure or greener technology. We propose a diagnostic lens that surfaces what efficiency-only evaluation hides, a step towards deciding how much computation is justified.

We plan to approach this gradually. Each step moves further from efficiency toward sufficiency, evaluating what it reveals, what data it demands, and what it trades away. The first step keeps the shape of a ranking but widens the impacts it accounts for. Beyond performance per watt alone, systems will be ranked according to the impact dimensions presented by the mapping. Carbon modelling already reaches both the operational and the manufacturing stage \parencite{li2023Sustainable}. Water can be estimated from the electricity a system draws and from the scarcity of the region supplying it \parencite{jiang2025ThirstyFLOPS}. The exercise is modest and near-term, since it needs only public Top500 entries and published impact factors. We expect that the rankings disagree once the impacts are introduced. A ranking on more dimensions is still a relative comparison, still efficiency thinking with a wider lens.

The second step will introduce sufficiency-oriented indicators, including, but not limited to, its total lifecycle burden, how heavily it is actually used, and how often it is replaced. They are also where the efficiency account and the sufficiency account diverge. The challenge of this step is data. Rao and Chien \parencite{rao2025Modeling} found a utilization figure for three of the 500 ranked systems, and essentially none of them report actual power draw, so we expect resorting to estimations and modelling. More than providing numbers, we intend to provide guidelines on how to make this information more accessible.

The third step turns the missing data into the object of study. Rather than rank systems only as they stand, we will test what the ranking becomes under a change: the same machine on a cleaner grid, with a newer generation of hardware, under a less water-intensive cooling design, or against a rising demand trajectory. The levers come from work already done, such as the balance between embodied and operational carbon that governs when hardware should be replaced \parencite{li2023Sustainable, uwizeyimana2025Carbonaware}. Each scenario supplies an assumed figure where the real one is unavailable, then shows how the re-ranking responds. We expect some levers to move a system's standing and others to barely register. The scenarios also let us argue for data the field does not yet publish, since they show what conclusions that data would support.

A boundary runs through all three steps. Public data supports part of this evaluation and not the rest. Peak performance and rated power are reported for most ranked systems, while embodied carbon per machine, the water intensity of a specific site, and true utilization are not. Rao and Chien \parencite{rao2025Modeling} mark roughly where the line falls, covering 98 percent of the Top500 for operational carbon once public sources beyond the list are added, and 80.8 percent for embodied carbon. We treat drawing that line as a result of its own. It marks how far a sufficiency-aware evaluation reaches on today's public record, and it turns the gap into a concrete request to the operators and vendors who could close it. The what-if step keeps the boundary from being a dead end, since it shows the payoff of each missing figure before anyone has measured it.

Taken together, the three steps move from a wider efficiency ranking toward an account of absolute burden. They rest on the same foundation as the rest of this thesis, the map of \Cref{ch:mapping} and the premise that HPC's impacts run past energy efficiency. The Green500 gave HPC a way to reward efficient machines. We aim to give the field a way to see what efficiency alone leaves out, and to make how much computing is enough part of how systems are judged.
